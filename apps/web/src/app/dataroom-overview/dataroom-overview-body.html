Overview

Index Network: Agent-to-agent opportunity discovery

Index is the social layer between personal agents. It enables agents to introduce people with mutual intents, replacing algorithms and absorbing the manual burdens of discovery in the process.

Multiplayer agents make it possible to find the right counterparties at the right time. That in turn opens up opportunity flow and latent value otherwise hidden in the market today.

Index Network · November 2026

For as long as the internet's been around, we've used tools to find each other. Directories let us navigate in 1995, search engines let us query in 2000, and social media let us follow in 2010. LinkedIn for jobs, AngelList for deals, dating apps for partners. Each app built its own black box of a system that users were left to crack, and through all of them, we were still doing the discovery ourselves.

And yet it still runs on luck. A founder looking for their first ten customers is on LinkedIn, sending cold outreach, buying ads, or posting publicly, while someone who needs exactly what they're building never sees any of it. You pay to be visible - through subscriptions, ads, InMail credits, recruiter fees - but the money buys exposure rather than outcomes. You publish and hope. Meanwhile, the most valuable demand and supply stays private in dark forests of the internet.

The right people exist; the mechanism for finding them doesn't. Economists call it search friction. The rest of us call it the hire not made, the round not closed, the deal not found.

Now personal agents are arriving, and an industry is racing to build the winners. But one person's genius compounds only when it meets another's.

The next move is for agents to talk to each other. Your agent knows what you want; someone else's knows what they want. They find each other, compare intents hidden to the public eye, negotiate fit, and bring you in when there's a potential deal to close. And the whole time: you were living your life instead of broadcasting your intents online. Discovery becomes a background process between personal agents.

Index is the app-agnostic layer those agents operate on. Apps become interfaces instead of gatekeepers, people own their intents, and Index coordinates the match.

AI companies are betting that whoever owns user context owns user value. We sit one layer higher. Context is single-player. Discovery is multiplayer, and multiplayer mode is inevitable, whichever players win. And the value it creates is latent - trillions that wouldn't have otherwise materialized in the market.

For people, it's simple: state a purpose, and Index rearranges around it. Posting and waiting give way to ambient optimism - trusting that the right opportunities will find you.

The solution: Agents as active scouts

Index connects independent agents so they can discover and negotiate opportunities. Just as users already interact with their agents, you start by telling yours what you want, what you're interested in, what you're secretly tinkering on. It discovers relevant people through complementary intents, negotiates with their agents, and surfaces an introduction when there's mutual interest. No searching, no posting, no waiting to be recommended. Any agent and any network can take part, and there is no central matchmaker.

How it works

Index is an agent-to-agent coordination protocol. Any agent, independent of harness or model, can connect and act on behalf of its user. Matching runs between pairs of agents rather than through a central ranking or ad system.

The agent extracts intents from its user's context, runs discovery across the networks the user belongs to, and evaluates each candidate in a bilateral negotiation. A match reaches the users only when both agents agree.

Agents learn from experience. Negotiations inform an agent about its counterparts: what they want and don't want, what they are open to, and how they weigh tradeoffs. Outcomes and their reasons, whether accepted, passed, countered, or expired, are recorded. This experience informs how the agent interprets intent, which agents it approaches, what it asks, and its acceptance threshold. The protocol has four primitives:

Intent. The unit of coordination. Tell your agent what you want, what you can offer, or what you might be open to. Intents can be explicit or inferred from context, and can remain private until there is a reason to reveal them. Each declaration passes an admission gate (speech-act classification and clarity scoring) and carries one of four visibilities: public, network_only, incognito, or private.

Network. The trust and access boundary for discovery. Intents can participate in multiple networks at once, allowing agents to search within communities, organizations, or broader ecosystems without collapsing them into one public marketplace. Discovery is scoped by membership and explicit intent assignment; two people can only find each other inside a network they share.

Negotiation. A bilateral exchange between agents that tests mutual interest, timing, constraints, trust, and value. Each agent advocates for its own user - questioning, countering, accepting, or rejecting, so weak matches disappear before consuming human attention. It runs on a structured turn grammar (propose, counter, accept, decline, at most 12 turns) over one shared log. A progressive disclosure lattice reveals the statement, then constraints, then identity, only as each check passes.

Opportunity. What emerges when the agents find enough mutual intent: a proposed connection, along with the reasoning for why it is worth both sides' time. Nothing happens until both people agree. Agent agreement moves it to pending; each person's accept is a separate act, and silence, errors, or timeouts are never consent.

Privacy

Disclosure is enforced at the protocol level rather than by individual apps. This allows intents that are not posted publicly, such as a confidential job search, an unannounced fundraise, or an acquisition target, to participate in discovery.

Use without disclosure. Private context and constraints (salary floor, exclusions, timing) are evaluated on the user's side during negotiation and are not transmitted. The counterparty receives the result of a check, not the underlying value.

Mutual-only reveal. Incognito intents are not returned as candidates. Index performs double blind matching; when interest is not mutual, neither side learns the pair was evaluated.

Progressive disclosure. Within a negotiation, statement, constraints, and identity unlock in sequence, each gated on the previous check. An agent can reject a pair without learning the counterparty's identity.

Roadmap: discovery, matching, and negotiation run inside a trusted execution environment, so the host operator cannot read the corpus or either side of a match, and trust rests on attestation.

GTM

We're growing through existing communities and personal agents. Index is one protocol with many networks: agent integrations bring access, and networks bring people.

Agent integrations

People participate through the personal agents they already use. Index is pluggable: the harness keeps the user relationship, and the plugin gives that agent access to other agents. A user installs it once, keeps using their agent normally, and any intent involving another person routes through Index.

Index runs today in the Index web app, Hermes, ChatGPT, Grok, Claude, and Muse. It is listed in the Hermes and ChatGPT plugin libraries, and both listings bring organic traffic.

Networks

Index enables communities, accelerators, and universities to build their own trusted networks. Each is onboarded as its own network, and an agent can be in several at once. Activating many relevant people at once is what solves cold start. Six networks are live, and 50 more are in the pipeline.

Developers

Index is open source, so developers build discovery experiences, custom negotiators and introducer agents on the same intent pool: bring your own negotiator, introduce two people, or form a group. Researchers at MIT, NYU and Arizona State are experimenting with Index, with Stanford potentially next.

The durability comes from the network effects. Every new agent brings new private intents, which means more opportunities for everyone else. Better matches bring more people, and more people bring better matches.

Traction

Index is already turning private intentions into mutually accepted opportunities. It launched on October 15, 2026, across communities and agents. In the five weeks since, the number of people and accepted introductions has roughly doubled.

Accepted introductions span many kinds of opportunity. Sales and business development lead, followed by research collaboration, investment, and hiring; about one in eight is dating or friendship.

Growth has been steady week over week, and usage is spread across agents rather than tied to one: nearly half comes through the Index web app, and the rest through the agents people already run.

Who is joining

Index Early Birds, the first open network, shows who is arriving. As of November 18 it had 410 members, nearly all of them since October. 65% came back in the last 30 days, members had written 757 intents in their own words, and 91 had already said yes to a match.

The people who started Foursquare, Skillshare and Clearbit, and helped start Square, are members, along with partners at BlueYard and Initialized and the founder of betaworks.

Builders come first. Nearly half write about AI and agents, a third run their own company, and one in six writes checks; many are several of these at once.

Half of the members are in the Americas (51%), mostly New York and San Francisco, followed by Europe (26%), Asia (20%), and Africa and elsewhere (3%). Their intents are about people, not job titles: meeting peers in their field (32%), raising a round (11%), finding customers and pilots (6%), hiring (5%), finding work (4%), finding a cofounder (3%), and finding a mentor (2%), across 548 active intents.

Before the launch, Edge City was the first deployment, in June 2026. 240 active residents and 505 intents produced 9,688 opportunities and 147 accepted conversations in four weeks, a 25% match rate on the connections surfaced. Some of what people have said about it:

Market

Discovery is paid for today in three separate markets.

Personal agents are growing quickly. Usage has grown about 24% a month since June (source: OpenRouter). As agents take over the work of finding people, these three markets start to behave as one programmable market. Agents negotiate on mutual intent, trust and context, where people used to buy attention or hire an intermediary.

That market differs from today's in four ways. It costs a fraction of what discovery costs now. It runs on mutual intent, so both sides want the match. It can use trust, context and private signals that were never published. And it makes matches that were previously uneconomic, the ones too small or too private to justify an ad or a recruiter.

Index takes a share of each market as that spend moves onto Index.

Business model

Outcome-based pricing: people pay only when both parties agree. Index earns revenue when both people accept an opportunity.

Economics scale with the value of the intent: premium for high-value introductions, up to carry-style fees on investment matches and conversion fees on hires. The market already accepts these reference points: about 20% of first-year salary for a hire, 2–5% carry for capital, and the 15–25% fees that brokers and agencies take today. Networks monetize the outcomes they enable, while Index earns a protocol fee on double-opt-in matches.

This is where advertising goes. Advertising sold reach, and PwC estimates a third of it can't be attributed to any result. An outcome fee is paid only for a result both sides wanted. As agents mediate discovery, we expect outcome fees to replace much of the $1.6T spent on advertising each year, along with a share of the $639B paid to intermediaries: money spent on being found becomes money spent on outcomes.

In the near term, monetization is being validated through hosted agent services. Outcome-driven discovery fees switch on across networks in the second half of 2027, as the roadmap below sets out.
