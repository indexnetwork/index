Index Network: The Social Discovery Protocol

The problem: Finding the right people today still runs on luck

Discovery today is broken in three ways:

You pay monopoly rents. To be found, you pay whatever the platform asks. Subscriptions, ad impressions, InMail credits, 20% recruiter fees. These prices exist because searching is costly, not because of the value delivered.
Paying rent doesn't get you success. The model is: publish and hope the right someone sees it, incentivizing a system of personal branding, engagement farming, algorithm dependence. Every additional post makes discovery worse for everyone else. And - it's still manual and frustrating. You do all the work yourself - searching, filtering, messaging, and following up, again and again.
It's public. Your best intents never enter the market. Being found requires being visible. So you self-censor and wait until ideas feel ready. The most valuable demand stays private or below market in closed networks, and the market for it simply doesn't exist.

While the right people exist, the mechanism for finding them doesn't. Economics calls this search friction.

And the result? The perfect investment, hire, or collaborator slips away because someone logged off early one day.

AI compounds the friction. Generated noise raises the cost of finding something relevant while decreasing societal trust that the internet's working in our favor. While it's also made better tooling, those tools operate in a system that still relies on one-sided posting and searching.

Finding people never got a protocol, and discovery stayed a black box inside platforms. You name it, there's a market for it. We use LinkedIn for jobs, dating apps for love, marketplaces for goods. Underneath it's one problem: two sides who can't find each other, and an intermediary working the gap.

Discovery remained operated by the application layer, directed by whatever operating protocol the platforms chose. Until now, there were no AI agent peers on both sides that could understand unstructured and private intents and autonomously negotiate mutual fit.

Personal agents tear up that system. Every person can now have an active representative capable of searching, reasoning, negotiating, and selectively revealing information on their behalf.

When two agents can negotiate a match openly, shared standards replace black boxes. Apps then become interfaces into a shared opportunity network. The application owns the experience; the user owns the intent; the protocol coordinates the match. All interfaces operate with the same intent, negotiation, and opportunity graphs underneath to govern data flows. Discovery therefore no longer needs a proprietary matchmaker; it becomes infrastructure.

While AI companies race to build better harnesses, on the bet that whoever owns user context owns user value. But knowing the user better is insufficient because most valuable outcomes require coordination with another person whose intent is compatible.

We're betting one layer higher. The intent graph turns personal context into outcomes by making visual the latent opportunity markets afforded by private mutual intents. Context is single-player, while discovery is multiplayer.

The solution: Agents as active scouts

Index is built for a future where the right opportunities find you.

Instead of searching, posting, or waiting for an algorithm to recommend someone, you tell your agent what you want. It discovers relevant people, negotiates with their agents, and only brings you in when there is something worth acting on.

Index is the discovery and coordination protocol underneath this interaction: a shared way for personal agents to express intent, find one another across networks, negotiate mutual fit, and surface opportunities for their principals.

It moves discovery from passive (post and wait) to active (your agent delivers offers to you). For example: An investor looking for the right founder now just says the word. Their agent returns relevant builders who are already intellectually aligned. This enables all the matches that previously got away: a company not founded, a round not closed, a hire not made. Index reveals that latent market value, and takes its fee from value that didn't exist before.

How it works

Discovery runs directly between two users' agents instead of a central algorithm or ads making recommendations. The components:

Intent: The unit of coordination. Tell your agent what you want, what you can offer, or what you might be open to. Intents can be explicit or inferred from context, and can remain private until there is a reason to reveal them.
Network: The trust and access boundary for discovery. Intents can participate in multiple networks at once, allowing agents to search within communities, organizations, or broader ecosystems without collapsing them into one public marketplace.
Negotiation: A bilateral exchange between agents that tests mutual interest, timing, constraints, trust, and value. Each agent advocates for its own user—questioning, countering, accepting, or rejecting, so weak matches disappear before consuming human attention.
Opportunity: What emerges when the agents find enough mutual intent: a proposed connection, along with the reasoning for why it is worth both sides' time. Nothing happens until both people agree.

Market

There is no single "discovery market." Instead, a search friction sits inside every market where two sides need to find each other, and entire industries exist to monetize the gap: online advertising (~700B), recruiting (~500B), deals, dating apps, recommendations (Amazon attributes ~35% of revenue to recommendations). The size of these markets is the size of the friction. That's what the world pays not to solve the problem, but to cope with it.

Three coping mechanisms, each a market of its own:

Buying exposure. Priced per impression, not per match. LinkedIn ads average just 1% engagement rates and cost ~$81 per lead. PwC estimates 1/3 of online ad spend is unattributable.
Buying intermediaries. Paying a human to run the search for you. Recruiters take ~20% of first-year salary per hire; placement agents take 1.5–2.5% of capital raised. The fee prices the friction: it's what a match costs when finding is manual.
Spending labor. Doing the search yourself, at payroll cost. SDR teams, sourcing associates, conference circuits, cold outreach. A $200M fund fields ~3,000 inbound inquiries to close ten deals; the screening is a full-time job that produces a 0.3% hit rate.

Business Model

The old web sold impressions and clicks - exposure, whether or not it led anywhere. Index takes a fee only when a connection happens.

Outcome-based discovery fee — the engine. As agentic workflows shift monetization from access and attention toward completed outcomes, discovery becomes part of the transaction itself, and can participate in the value it creates. Networks monetize the outcomes they enable, while Index earns a protocol fee on double-opt-in matches. Economics scale with the value of the intent: premium for high-value introductions, up to carry-style fees on investment matches and conversion fees on hires. (Reference points already accepted by the market: ≈20% of first-year salary for a hire, ≈2–5% carry for capital.)
Negotiation cost — the spam filter. A small fee to pursue a negotiation, priced to make spam unprofitable. Declaring intents is free; pursuing someone costs. Money as proof of seriousness keeps the signal clean.
Infrastructure / inference. Index currently subsidizes inference and protocol infrastructure to maximize intent liquidity and grow the graph. Over time, networks pay usage-based infrastructure fees tied to agent traffic and compute consumption.

Money spent on being found moves to money spent on outcomes as agents mediate discovery.
