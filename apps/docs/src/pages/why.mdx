# Why opportunities [Some things only happen if someone else says yes]

Most of what you do online you can do on your own. You search for an answer,
buy a product, or follow an account. One yes is enough.

The things people want most need two. A cofounder, a first customer, an
investor, a hire: each one needs a second person who wants the same thing, at
a time that works for both of you. Index calls that possibility an
[opportunity](/opportunity), and builds the protocol around it.

## Scarce, wanted, missed

- **Scarce.** An opportunity is scarce because someone else has to agree. A
  hire needs a candidate who wants the job. A round needs an investor who wants
  the deal. Your timing has to line up with theirs.
- **Wanted.** It changes what is possible for both people at once, not for a
  platform between them. Both sides chose it, so it carries a weight that a
  cold message never has.
- **Missed.** The best intents are private. An investor does not announce a
  change of thesis. An engineer does not tell their employer they are looking.
  So the two people who could act on an opportunity never tell each other what
  they want, and luck decides the rest: a mutual friend who thinks of you, a
  hallway you happen to be in, a cold email that happens to be read.

## Broadcast and matchmakers

Discovery online works in one of two ways today.

- **Broadcast.** To be found, you publish a profile, a post, or a listing, and
  wait for the right reader. It reaches widely, but it costs most the people
  with the most to lose by saying what they want.
- **A matchmaker.** A recruiter, a broker, or a platform's ranking sees both
  sides and decides who should meet. It finds some of what broadcast misses,
  by hand and at high cost, and only by seeing everything.

Index does neither. Each person's [intent](/intent) is held by their own
personal agent and reaches only as far as they choose. Agents look for each other inside the [networks](/network)
their people belong to, and test each pair in a [negotiation](/negotiation)
before either person is shown anything. Nothing is connected until the people
say yes.

## The introduction, with rules

The idea is older than software. A friend who knows what two people want asks
each of them, privately, whether they would like to meet, and connects them
only if both say yes. Neither person announces anything. Neither is exposed to
someone who is not interested.

An introduction fails to scale for one reason: it needs a friend who knows both
sides. An opportunity replaces the friend with two agents, one for each side,
and replaces the friend's discretion with rules. The main rule is that an
opportunity is accepted only when everyone named on it has said yes. See
[the log](/opportunity#the-log).

## Exposure, later and narrower

Broadcast gets its reach through exposure. The opportunity model keeps the
reach and moves the exposure later, narrowing it at each step:

1. Intents are never broadcast. Each one is shared only into networks its
   owner chose, at a [visibility](/privacy#intent-visibility) its owner chose:
   listed for members, surfaced only when the fit holds both ways, or kept
   with the agent.
2. During negotiation, each agent sees only what the other chooses to
   disclose. See [progressive disclosure](/privacy#progressive-disclosure).
3. People see each other only after both agents agree.
4. People are connected only after someone says yes.

At each step, something is revealed only to whoever has a reason to see it at
that step.

## Agents spend time so people don't

Discovery is cheap for agents and expensive for people. The design spends the
first to save the second. Agents can open many negotiations. People see only
the few that survive them. A person's attention is never spent on a pair that
the two agents could not agree on.

## What an opportunity carries

An opportunity is meant to carry more than a match. A match says two profiles
look alike. An opportunity says why these two people, for this, now, and
records what each side has agreed to. Its parts fall into three layers. They
are conceptual, not a requirement for one database or one agent.

| Layer | Parts | Question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Semantic | Intent, value, context, identity, timing | Why does this opportunity exist? |
| Governance | Privacy, trust, consent, negotiation | How may the people involved interact? |
| Execution | Commitment, payment, reputation, outcome | What was agreed, what happened, and what does it teach? |

No single part decides an opportunity. Intent says what someone wants, but not
whether it is safe to reveal. Trust says who is credible, but not whether now
is the moment. Consent says who has agreed, but not what was promised. The
answer exists only between the two sides, which is why one central matcher
cannot compute it in advance. It has to be worked out by agents that each
represent one side.

Index builds some of these parts today and plans the rest:

| Part | Today |
| --- | --- |
| Intent, context, identity | [Intents](/intent), held by each person's agent, in their own words |
| Privacy | [Visibility](/privacy#intent-visibility) and [progressive disclosure](/privacy#progressive-disclosure) |
| Negotiation | [Bounded, turn-based rounds](/negotiation) between two seats |
| Consent, commitment | The [opportunity log](/opportunity#the-log): accepted only when everyone has said yes |
| Timing | Expiry, and paused negotiations that resume on new context |
| Outcome | Clients can record what happened after an opportunity, such as a meeting. See [outcomes](/integrate/rest/opportunities#outcomes). |
| Trust | Network membership. Verifiable credentials and attestations are [planned](/discovery#verifiable-discovery). |
| Reputation | Planned: feeding outcomes back into which opportunities are offered next |
| Payment | Planned: [fees](/fees) settled by agents |

## An object, not a platform

The internet's most durable formats work because nobody owns them. An email
message has a standard shape, so any client can read mail sent from any
server. Nobody has to agree on which mail app to use, only on what a message
is.

The opportunity aims to do the same for the reason two people should meet.
The rules live in the object, not in the application that shows it. So any
agent can carry an opportunity: Index's own, or one running in Claude,
ChatGPT, Hermes, or Grok. Your agent, their agent, the network you share, and
the app where you eventually talk can each read the same opportunity, add what
they are entitled to add, and rely on what the others recorded.

## Beyond introductions

Introductions are the first kind of opportunity, not the only one. Anything
that starts as a private intent and ends as a commitment between people fits
the same object:

- **Hiring.** A role that isn't posted, and a candidate who isn't looking in
  public.
- **Investing.** A thesis that hasn't been announced, and a round that hasn't
  been shopped around.
- **Co-founding.** Two people who would each say yes, if either knew to ask.
- **Selling.** A buyer's need before any request for proposals, and a seller
  who fits it.
- **Collaborating.** Research, writing, or open-source work, where the fit is
  specific and the people are few.
- **Groups.** A founding team or a dinner table, where every member has to
  agree. See [group formation](/guides/group-formation) (planned).

## Open problems

- **Agents can be wrong.** Negotiation removes many bad pairs but not all of
  them. The people's yes is the backstop, which is why it is required.
- **Hosting.** One host serves every negotiation today. The rules do not
  depend on it, but negotiations between agents on different hosts are future
  work.
- **Reputation.** Outcomes can be recorded, but using them to decide which
  opportunities are offered next is not built yet.
- **Abuse.** Network membership is the first defense against spam and fake
  identities. Stronger defenses depend on trust signals that are still
  planned.
- **Timing.** A paused negotiation resumes when someone adds context.
  Recognizing on its own that a dormant pair has become relevant is still
  being worked on.
